Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Pressure, Overconfidence, or Neither? Ball-by-Ball Evidence on Dismissal Risk and Batting Aggression in Twenty20 Cricket", for publication in the *Journal of Sports Economics*.

Players and commentators widely believe that a dot ball builds pressure and that a boundary breeds overconfidence, and that both lead to wickets. The manuscript tests these beliefs with every delivery bowled in eleven seasons of the Indian Premier League. It makes three contributions. First, it shows that the retrospective evidence that appears to support the belief comes from sample selection. Second, it shows that, once the match state is held fixed, the striker's previous ball does not predict dismissal. Equivalence tests rule out effects larger than a tenth of the base rate. Third, it documents a robust behavioural response: boundary rates rise after a dot and after a six. A within-innings permutation test that corrects for the finite-sequence bias identified by Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) shows that the response to a dot, and most of the response to a six, reflects the order of balls rather than the conditions on the day.

The paper adds to the journal's literature on performance under pressure and on momentum in sport, using a setting in which pressure is frequent and low-stakes rather than concentrated in single decisive events. All data are public, and the replication package reproduces every table, figure and number in the text from the raw files with a single command.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, and the author has no conflicts of interest to declare.
